==============================================================================
DECISION THRESHOLD CALIBRATION
==============================================================================
Active policy: minimise expected rupee loss subject to recall >= 90%,
pricing a missed fraud at its transaction amount and a false alarm at Rs.150.
Calibrated on 5-fold out-of-fold probabilities over train+validation
(~400 positives), never on the test set.

  Shipped threshold         : 0.1541
  Precision-at-recall point : 0.5293  (recall 0.9050, precision 0.9211 out-of-fold)
  Cost-optimal point        : 0.1541  (recall 1.0000, expected loss Rs.25,650)
  F1-optimal threshold      : 0.4755 (F1 = 0.9210)
  Single-split alternative  : 0.5488  (what the 80-positive validation split alone would have suggested)
  Default baseline          : 0.50

  Out-of-fold comparison (400 fraud rows, the largest sample available):
                                Precision   Recall       F1   Alarms  Missed   Expected loss
    default 0.50                   0.8998   0.9200   0.9098       41      32    Rs.1,352,433
    precision-at-recall 0.5293     0.9211   0.9050   0.9130       31      38    Rs.1,666,331
    cost-optimal 0.1541            0.7005   1.0000   0.8239      171       0       Rs.25,650

  Why the two policies disagree, and why 'cost' is the one shipping:
  the 90% recall floor sits below what this model achieves, so
  'maximise precision' is free to climb the threshold until it hits the floor.
  Out-of-fold that buys +22.1% precision and +140 alarms,
  at the price of +38 fraud rows let through.
  Priced in rupees the trade is negative by Rs.1,640,681: a missed mule
  transfer costs its full amount, a false alarm costs one Rs.150 review.
  Active policy is 'cost' (set THRESHOLD_POLICY at the top of this
  file to switch). Raising TARGET_RECALL has much the same effect.

==============================================================================
MERCHANT ECONOMICS (test set)
==============================================================================
  A bank pays a fixed sum to review an alert. A merchant declining a good
  customer loses the margin on that order plus the cost of winning them back,
  so the false-positive cost scales with basket size. And a gateway has a
  third action a bank does not: challenge the payment with a step-up.

  Assumptions   chargeback fee Rs.1,250  |  contribution margin 18%  |  goodwill Rs.400
                step-up abandon 8%  |  step-up catch 85%  |  manual review Rs.150
                step-up budget 10% of payments

  Policy                                Total cost   Per txn    Held  Stepped up  Fraud through
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  block/allow at 0.1541             Rs.    244,672     12.23     130           -              1
  accept / step-up / hold           Rs.    180,472      9.02      75       1,231              0

  Challenge budget used  : 6.16% of 10% (headroom left)
  The budget is not decoration. Minimising cost row by row, a step-up is so
  cheap that the policy will challenge anything carrying more than a fraction
  of a percent of risk - on a book with diffuse scores that reached 94% of
  legitimate traffic in testing. Arithmetically optimal, and it would destroy
  conversion. Friction is a portfolio resource, so it is capped and then spent
  on the payments where a challenge saves the most.

  Adding the challenge action saves Rs.64,201 on 20,000 payments (26% of merchant loss),
  by moving 1,231 payments off the analyst queue and onto a
  step-up that costs a slice of conversion instead of the whole order. Manual
  holds fall from 130 to 75.

  Card-network dispute covenant (Visa VDMP / Mastercard ECP):
    Expected disputes      : 4.5 of 20,000 payments
    Dispute ratio          : 0.0225%  (ceiling 0.90%)
    Within covenant        : yes

  Being straight about this constraint: at 0.5% fraud prevalence the covenant
  is slack by construction - even letting every fraud through would sit under
  the 0.9% ceiling. It starts to bind above 11.3% prevalence at the 92%
  recall the age-ablated model achieves, which is the regime a compromised
  merchant category actually lives in. It is reported because it is the real
  operating limit, not because it is doing work on this dataset.

SPLIT INTEGRITY - do scam incidents straddle train and test?
==============================================================================
  Test fraud rows whose receiver VPA also appears in training fraud: 0/100 (0.0%)
  Recall on 'cold' receivers (100 rows, never seen): 99.00%

  A stratified random split cuts through scam incidents - a Rs.1 probe can sit in
  train while its large follow-up sits in test, and one mule VPA is scattered across
  both. The cold-receiver recall is the fairer estimate for a brand-new scam ring.
  Switching to a group-aware split (grouped on receiver_vpa) would remove the effect
  entirely; the stratified split was kept because it is what the module specifies.

ABLATION - how much rests on receiver VPA age?
==============================================================================
  Dropped: receiver_vpa_age_days, log_receiver_vpa_age, is_new_receiver_vpa, is_recent_receiver_vpa
                            PR-AUC    Recall   Precision        F1
  Full feature set          0.9802    0.9900      0.7615    0.8609
  Without VPA age           0.8788    0.9200      0.5860    0.7160

  Every fraudulent receiver in the synthetic data is 0-20 days old, so this
  feature will always carry real signal here. Module 1 deliberately routes a
  slice of ordinary traffic - and a thin tail of genuine big-ticket payments -
  to brand-new VPAs, so account age is no longer separable on its own: the
  rule 'receiver is 20 days old or younger' is only ~7% precise on this data.
  The gap above is what the temporal, velocity and VPA-structure features
  deliver alone, and is the better guide to behaviour on real traffic.
